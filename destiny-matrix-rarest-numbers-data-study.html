The Rarest Destiny Matrix Numbers — a Data Study

We counted every birth date from 1 January 1900 to 31 December 2100 — 73,414 dates — through the same calculation this site uses, and wrote down how often each centre actually occurs. Some centres are ten times more common than others, and two never occur at all.

What we counted, and how

Every date from 1 January 1900 to 31 December 2100 was run through this site's own calculation: day is A, month is B, year digit sum is C, and centre is reduce22(A+B+C). That is 73,414 dates, counted in two independent implementations, Python and JavaScript, which agreed on every one. The method is public on the methodology page, and the code is the same code that runs the free calculator, so anyone can reproduce the count. It is an exhaustive count.

The headline: centres 1 and 2 never occur

The most surprising result is also the simplest: centres 1 and 2 occur in 0.00% of birth dates. The centre is the reduced sum of three numbers, and the smallest that sum can be is 3, so the Magician and the High Priestess can never sit at the centre of a chart on this site. That is arithmetic, not a bug — and sums cluster in the middle.

The most common centre, and the rarest

The most common centre is 5, the Hierophant, at 10.20% of birth dates — about one person in ten. Close behind are 6, the Lovers, at 9.97%, and 7, the Chariot, at 9.70%. The rarest centre that actually occurs is 14, Temperance, at 0.91% — about one person in 110. Next rarest are 13, Death, at 1.17%, and 15, the Devil, at 1.15%. Centres 5 to 11 together cover 65.63% of all birth dates, while centres 12 to 22 together cover only 23.21%. If your centre is 14, your chart is genuinely unusual; if it is 5, it is the most ordinary result in the system.

Why the numbers are not equal

The reason is the same reason dice sums cluster around seven: there are many more ways to make a middle sum than an extreme one. A centre of 5 can come from dozens of combinations, while a centre of 14 needs a rarer one, and a centre of 1 or 2 needs an impossible one. The year digit sum is the hidden driver: years like 2000 (digit sum 2) push centres down, while years like 1999 (digit sum 28, reduced to 10) push them up.

How the frequencies drift by decade

Centre 8, Justice, was 10.93% of birth dates in the 1930s and only 6.84% in the 2000s, before climbing back to 10.60% in the 2090s. Centre 22, the Fool, moved the other way: 1.48% in the 1930s, 5.15% in the 2000s and 2010s, then back down to 2.38% in the 2090s. Centre 3, the Empress, fell from 5.15% in the 1900s to 3.28% in the 2000s, then rose again to 5.15% in the 2080s. The drift follows the calendar.

What this study does, and does not, prove

This study proves one thing: how often each centre occurs, under one stated method, across 201 years. It does not prove that a centre means anything about a person, or that a rare centre is better or worse than a common one. Rarity here is a fact about arithmetic, not about people.
